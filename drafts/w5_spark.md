Week 5 of 10 - Writing with AI

---

For four weeks now, you've been using AI to produce text for you - summaries, syntheses, critiques, stress tests. You've gotten more specific with your prompts, grounded the outputs in your own sources, and learned to check what comes back ... and thus learned how to write well *for AI.*

This week turns the lens around. What happens when AI helps with *your* writing?

If you've been anywhere near an LLM in the last year, you've probably done it already: pasted a paragraph into ChatGPT and asked it to "improve this." Or "make this clearer." Or "rewrite this for a general audience." The output comes back polished, fluent, and oddly unfamiliar. It's smoother than what you wrote. But is it still yours?

This is evidently a huge question in higher education when it comes to learning and assessment. But let's not go there. For now, the focus is on research, where things are complex enough.

**Writing as thinking**

Before we get into the quality of AI-assisted writing, let's be real. For most of us, writing is hard *not* because typing is hard - but because we don't quite know yet what to say. The act of writing isn't just the production of text. It's a mode of thinking. When you struggle to explain something clearly, that is often because you don't fully understand it yet. In the struggle to explain it, insight can happen - so deleting text is not wasted effort, but often a subtle sign of progress. The version that finally works usually captures something you didn't quite understand until you worked through it.

This isn't a luddite argument. It's a cognitive one. Decades of research on writing-to-learn suggest that the effortful process of translating ideas into prose forces a kind of elaboration that passive understanding doesn't require. You have to decide what's essential, what order things go in, which caveats actually matter. When you hand that process to an AI, you might get better text. But you skip the part where you actually work through what you think.

Whether that matters depends very much on the function of the text, and your personal priorities. For certain parts of a grant application, or a report, it might be sufficient to have clear appropriate text on the page. For something that you have already fully understood but need to rewrite yet again, there might be no benefit in crafting sentences yourself again.

So, not every paragraph needs to be wrung out by hand. Routine writing (emails, administrative text, standard methods descriptions) doesn't carry much cognitive load, and sometimes the entirely blank page might just paralyse rather than inspire. The issue is with the writing where your thinking actually develops: the introduction that frames your contribution, the discussion that works out what your findings mean, the paragraph where you articulate *why* your approach differs from existing work. That's where outsourcing the writing risks outsourcing the thinking - and where the outsourced thinking will still remain rather shallow.

**Editing vs generating**

Not all AI writing assistance is the same, though. The distinction between editing and generating matters more than most people realise.

When AI edits your draft - flagging unclear logic, pointing out a missing transition, suggesting you've buried your main point three paragraphs in - it's acting as a reader. You wrote the ideas. You chose the structure. The AI is telling you where a reader might get lost. That keeps you in the driver's seat, and is frankly something that I personally expect writers to do these days. Not taking that simple step to improve the quality of drafts can be frustrating for reviewers, supervisors and other recipients.

However, when AI generates your draft - producing arguments, framing your contribution, interpreting your findings - it's doing something fundamentally different. The ideas are assembled by a system that doesn't share *your* perspective on your field, and that hasn't selected the sources *you* want to prioritise. It will have read most of them, but buried amidst billions of words from across the whole spectrum of quality and relevance. So it can't distinguish between a finding you're confident about and one you think is shaky. The text may be fluent, but the thinking is unlikely to be original, or capable of pushing your own thinking.

The line between editing and generating isn't always clean. "Rewrite this paragraph for clarity" can fall on either side depending on how much the AI restructures the argument. There can also be cycles. I often co-write with AI, where I generate ideas, refine them in dialogue with AI, then write out bullet points and might let an AI turn them into prose, then edit that and get another round of feedback. That is how most of the materials for this course came about. When I have the expertise and energy to stay in the driver seat, this works well - but I have also found myself leaning back too far at times.


There is a test I find useful: after the AI has helped, can you still explain *why* the text says what it says? If you can, the thinking is still yours. If the AI introduced a framing you hadn't considered and you just accepted it because it sounded good, you've crossed too far into generation territory.

**The composite voice problem**

There's another cost to heavy AI editing that's harder to pin down: voice.

AI prose tends toward a specific register. Articulate, balanced, slightly formal, aggressively smooth. It sounds professional. It also sounds like everyone else's AI-edited text. Some words ("delve", "tapestry", "pivotal") and even the em dash itself have become recognisable tells. And that may matter at scale: if enough researchers run their writing through the same tools, academic prose could converge toward a generic median. Our voice - the specific way we frame problems, the hedges we choose, the examples we reach for - is part of our scholarly identity. That might become less central if more text is generated, but losing it would appear a real loss.

However, a *part* of that is related to the homogenisation of ideas, and we already considered some ways to avoid that back in week 3. If everyone gives the same prompt to the same model, outcomes will be bland. Once we start making it our own, and specifically ask for what *we* want, the problem becomes smaller. For instance, when working with AI on this text, I have an extensive style guide in place, that I partly wrote, and that AI partly drafted based on looking at previous teaching materials I had created. I would hope that makes it less generic.

Keeping our distinctive voice isn't about clinging to bad habits. If your writing is genuinely unclear, AI editing can help. As a German, I tend to write rather long sentences when left to my own devices. Trying to break through that for this medium seemed worthwhile, and was a lot easier with persistent AI reminders. However, the concern is about what happens when "clarity" becomes a synonym for "sounds like an LLM." It should mean that it sounds like a polished version of our individual styles - but that requires deliberate prompting, and some restraint.

**A two-pass approach**

I already said that I sometimes write quite iteratively with AI, particularly when I have a good sense of what I want to say (like here). However, for more technical writing, a more deliberate approach can work better when you split the process into two distinct passes.

**Pass 1: Structural and logical critique.** Ask the AI to read your draft and identify problems, but explicitly tell it not to rewrite anything. "Where are the logical gaps? Which transitions are unclear? What claims need more support? Don't rewrite, just tell me what's not working." This gives you the benefit of a fresh reader without surrendering the writing. You still have to figure out how to fix the problems, and that figuring-out is where the thinking happens. For me, such thinking often happens in dialogue with an LLM ... but then focused on content, not style.

**Pass 2: Style smoothing with constraints.** After you've revised based on the structural feedback, you can ask for stylistic suggestions with guardrails. "Suggest edits for clarity and flow. Don't introduce new claims. Highlight any changes that alter meaning so I can review them." This keeps the AI in an editorial role rather than a generative one. If you work with AI agents (which we will discuss from next week onwards) this becomes easier as they can edit your text directly (no more copy-pasting) *while changes remain transparent.*

The point of separating these passes isn't that one is valuable and the other isn't. Both help. But they engage different cognitive processes, and mixing them up - asking the AI to "improve" your draft in one go - usually means it does too much generating, and you surrender understanding. Just asking AI to "improve this" is likely to produce text that sounds great, while it remains shallow and isn't really yours.

## Try this: two-pass writing test (10-12 min)

Take a paragraph from something you're currently working on. A draft paper, a grant application, even a detailed email to a collaborator. Something where the quality of the thinking matters, not just the polish.

**Pass 1 (3-4 min):** Paste the paragraph into a chat LLM with this prompt: "Read this paragraph and give me a logic-only critique. Where are the logical connections weak or missing? Where might a reader get confused? Don't rewrite anything - just identify problems." If you can, provide the full paper as context - just as an attached file. That is generally good practice when using AI for editing. Asking for feedback on a single paragraph without context cannot really yield results that go beyond the surface.

Read the critique. Then revise the paragraph yourself based on what you find useful.

**Pass 2 (3-4 min):** Take your revised version and paste it back in with: "Now suggest style edits for clarity and flow. No new claims."

Compare three versions: your original, your revision after the logic critique, and the AI's style-smoothed version.

- Which pass produced more useful feedback?
- Did the logic critique help you think, or just confirm what you already knew?
- In the style pass, did the AI change anything that altered what you meant?
- Which version sounds most like you?

If you don't have a work-in-progress paragraph handy, take a paragraph from something you've already published and run it through both passes. Sometimes seeing what the AI would change in your finished work is more revealing than testing it on a rough draft.

**Journal prompt**

Where in your writing process is AI most helpful so far? Where could you experiment a bit more/be more deliberate? Where does it get in the way of your thinking?
