Week 5 of 10 - Writing with AI

---

For four weeks now, you've been using AI to produce text for you - summaries, syntheses, critiques, stress tests. You've gotten more specific with your prompts, grounded the outputs in your own sources, and learned to check what comes back.

This week turns the lens around. What happens when AI helps with *your* writing?

If you've been anywhere near an LLM in the last year, you've probably done it already: pasted a paragraph into ChatGPT and asked it to "improve this." Or "make this clearer." Or "rewrite this for a general audience." The output comes back polished, fluent, and oddly unfamiliar. It's smoother than what you wrote. But is it still yours?

**Writing as thinking**

Here's the thing that gets lost in conversations about AI-assisted writing: the act of writing isn't just the production of text. It's a mode of thinking. When you struggle to explain something clearly, that struggle often *is* where the insight happens. The sentence you rewrite six times isn't wasted effort. The version that finally works usually captures something you didn't quite understand until you worked through it.

This isn't a luddite argument. It's a cognitive one. Decades of research on writing-to-learn suggest that the effortful process of translating ideas into prose forces a kind of elaboration that passive understanding doesn't require. You have to decide what's essential, what order things go in, which caveats actually matter. When you hand that process to an AI, you might get better text. But you skip the part where you actually work through what you think.

That doesn't mean every paragraph needs to be wrung out by hand. Routine writing (emails, administrative text, standard methods descriptions) doesn't carry much cognitive load. The issue is with the writing where your thinking actually develops: the introduction that frames your contribution, the discussion that works out what your findings mean, the paragraph where you articulate why your approach differs from existing work. That's where outsourcing the writing risks outsourcing the thinking.

**Editing vs generating**

Not all AI writing assistance is the same, though. The distinction between editing and generating matters more than most people realise.

When AI edits your draft - flagging unclear logic, pointing out a missing transition, suggesting you've buried your main point three paragraphs in - it's acting as a reader. You wrote the ideas. You chose the structure. The AI is telling you where a reader might get lost. That keeps you in the driver's seat.

When AI generates your draft - producing arguments, framing your contribution, interpreting your findings - it's doing something fundamentally different. The ideas are assembled by a system that doesn't understand your field, hasn't read the papers you've read, and can't distinguish between a finding you're confident about and one you think is shaky. The text may be fluent. The thinking is borrowed.

The line between editing and generating isn't always clean. "Rewrite this paragraph for clarity" can fall on either side depending on how much the AI restructures the argument. But paying attention to which side you're on is worth the effort. A useful test: after the AI has helped, can you still explain *why* the text says what it says? If you can, the thinking is still yours. If the AI introduced a framing you hadn't considered and you just accepted it because it sounded good, you've crossed into generation territory.

**The composite voice problem**

There's another cost to heavy AI editing that's harder to pin down: voice.

AI prose tends toward a specific register. Articulate, balanced, slightly formal, aggressively smooth. It sounds professional. It also sounds like everyone else's AI-edited text. If enough researchers run their writing through the same tools, academic prose starts to converge toward a generic median. Your voice - the specific way you frame problems, the hedges you choose, the examples you reach for - is part of your scholarly identity. It's how colleagues recognise your thinking on the page.

This isn't about clinging to bad habits. If your writing is genuinely unclear, AI editing can help. The concern is about what happens when "clarity" becomes a synonym for "sounds like an LLM." I've noticed this in my own editing: I ask AI to smooth a sentence, and the result is technically better but sounds like it could have been written by anyone. The rough edges I'd ironed out were part of how I think.

**A two-pass approach**

So how do you get the genuine benefits of AI editing without losing cognitive ownership?

One approach that works well is to separate the process into two distinct passes.

**Pass 1: Structural and logical critique.** Ask the AI to read your draft and identify problems, but explicitly tell it not to rewrite anything. "Where are the logical gaps? Which transitions are unclear? What claims need more support? Don't rewrite, just tell me what's not working." This gives you the benefit of a fresh reader without surrendering the writing. You still have to figure out how to fix the problems, and that figuring-out is where the thinking happens.

**Pass 2: Style smoothing with constraints.** After you've revised based on the structural feedback, you can ask for stylistic suggestions with guardrails. "Suggest edits for clarity and flow. Don't introduce new claims. Highlight any changes that alter meaning so I can review them." This keeps the AI in an editorial role rather than a generative one.

The point of separating these passes isn't that one is valuable and the other isn't. Both help. But they engage different cognitive processes, and mixing them up - asking the AI to "improve" your draft in one go - usually means it does too much generating and not enough editing. The combined "improve this" prompt is the one most likely to produce text that sounds great and isn't really yours.

## Try this: two-pass writing test (10-12 min)

Take a paragraph from something you're currently working on. A draft paper, a grant application, even a detailed email to a collaborator. Something where the quality of the thinking matters, not just the polish.

**Pass 1 (3-4 min):** Paste the paragraph into a chat LLM with this prompt: "Read this paragraph and give me a logic-only critique. Where are the logical connections weak or missing? Where might a reader get confused? Don't rewrite anything - just identify problems."

Read the critique. Then revise the paragraph yourself based on what you find useful.

**Pass 2 (3-4 min):** Take your revised version and paste it back in with: "Now suggest style edits for clarity and flow. No new claims. Highlight any changes that alter the meaning so I can review them."

Compare three versions: your original, your revision after the logic critique, and the AI's style-smoothed version.

- Which pass produced more useful feedback?
- Did the logic critique help you think, or just confirm what you already knew?
- In the style pass, did the AI change anything that altered what you meant?
- Which version sounds most like you?

If you don't have a work-in-progress paragraph handy, take a paragraph from something you've already published and run it through both passes. Sometimes seeing what the AI would change in your finished work is more revealing than testing it on a rough draft.
