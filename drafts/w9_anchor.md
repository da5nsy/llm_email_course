Week 9 of 10 - Governance and disclosure

If you've been paying attention to AI developments beyond this course, you've probably noticed a background hum of anxiety alongside the practical benefits. Each new model does things the previous one couldn't. Tasks that seemed safely human keep getting automated. The question lurking underneath isn't just "how do I use these tools?" It's "what happens to my work - and my role - if they keep getting better?"

This is worth naming directly, because most AI courses don't. The anxiety is understandable. It's also more useful as a prompt to think carefully about your role than as a reason to either panic or dismiss the trend.

**The honest version**

Nobody knows where this goes. The people predicting AI will replace researchers within five years and the people predicting it will plateau next year are both guessing. The trajectory has surprised experts in both directions, repeatedly.

On Thursday last week, we looked at which specific tasks stay human and which you might delegate. The question here is broader: not which tasks, but what makes you valuable as a researcher in a world where execution keeps getting cheaper.

What seems durable isn't a list of tasks but a set of roles. *Asking the right questions* - spotting a genuine gap, designing a study nobody thought to run - still depends on the kind of sustained engagement with a field that no model currently has. *Building unique access* - running experiments, cultivating relationships that open doors to restricted data, conducting fieldwork - becomes more valuable as analysis gets cheap. *Bridge-building* across disciplines and audiences, connecting a statistical finding to a policy debate or explaining technical work to a community that needs it. And *orchestration* - designing a research programme, choosing which questions to pursue, coordinating people and tools toward a coherent goal.

The common thread isn't "things AI can't do" (that list keeps shrinking). It's judgment, access, and the ability to care about getting it right, not just getting it done. Researchers who learn to orchestrate AI effectively don't become less relevant. They become more productive at the parts of research that actually matter.

That said, this isn't a comfortable guarantee that nothing will change. Some tasks that feel core to your identity as a researcher will be automated. Sitting with that discomfort, rather than resolving it prematurely in either direction, is probably the honest position.

**Risks worth following**

Briefly: deskilling is a real concern. If we outsource learning too fast, we lose the ability to catch errors. Aviation learned this with automated cockpits - overall safer, but new failure modes when pilots couldn't take over during edge cases. The parallel to research isn't exact, but the pattern is worth noticing. Meanwhile, AI-generated slop is already increasing noise in published literature, which degrades the knowledge base everyone relies on [NEEDS EVIDENCE]. These aren't reasons to stop using AI. They're reasons the thoughtful practices from this course actually matter.

**Staying current without burning out**

New tools appear constantly. Most are incremental - a new provider, a slightly better benchmark. Paradigm shifts are rarer: transformers, chat interfaces, reasoning modes, agents. The useful test: does this change what's *possible*, or just who provides it?

For staying oriented without drowning in hype, find 2-3 sources you trust and ignore the noise. Ethan Mollick's *One Useful Thing* newsletter covers practical AI-at-work developments well. The FORRT community tracks open science intersections. Your field's methods journals will flag discipline-specific developments when they matter.

## Try this: quarterly calibration (5-8 min)

1. Choose two benchmark tasks from your work. One where AI is already reliable for you. One where it currently struggles or you don't trust it.
2. For each, define what counts as "pass" - specific, checkable criteria.
3. Put a quarterly reminder in your calendar to re-test with the best available tools.
4. When a "struggles" task moves to "pass," update your SOP.

**Journal prompt**

Which capabilities do you expect to change most in the next year? What would make you update your practice?
