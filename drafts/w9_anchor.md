This one's longer than usual - we're nearing the end and there are things I want to say that don't fit neatly into a 5-minute read.

If you've been paying attention to AI developments beyond this course, you've probably noticed a background hum of anxiety alongside the practical benefits. Each new model does things the previous one couldn't. Tasks that seemed safely human keep getting automated. The question lurking underneath isn't just "how do I use these tools?" It's "what happens to my work, and my role, if they keep getting better?"

Here, I don't have strong guidance to offer. When seeing that progress, excitement indeed sometimes comes with angst. But that is a reason to think about our roles in academia - not to panic or become defensive.

**What the future holds**

Nobody knows. The people predicting AI will replace researchers within five years and the people predicting it will plateau next year are both guessing. The trajectory has surprised experts positively and negatively, repeatedly.

On Thursday last week, we looked at which specific tasks stay human and which you might delegate. The question here is broader: not which tasks, but what makes *you* valuable as a researcher in a world where execution keeps getting cheaper.

What seems durable isn't a list of tasks but a set of roles. 

- *Asking the right questions* - spotting a genuine gap, designing a study nobody thought to run - still depends on the kind of sustained engagement with a field that no model currently has. It also depends on *your* motivations for creating knowledge, as in your epistemic and social values and motivations. 
- *Building unique access* - running experiments in the real world, cultivating relationships that open doors to restricted data, conducting fieldwork - becomes more valuable as analysis gets cheap. 
- *Bridge-building* across disciplines and audiences, connecting a statistical finding to a policy debate or explaining technical work to a community that needs it. Models can support this when prompted, but will not *spot* the need for such bridge-building.
- And *orchestration* - designing a research programme, choosing which questions to pursue, coordinating people and tools toward a coherent goal.

The common thread isn't "things AI can't do" (that list keeps shrinking). It's judgment, access, and the ability to care about getting it right, not just getting it done. Researchers who learn to orchestrate AI effectively don't become less relevant. Instead, we will hopefully gain time for the parts of research that truly matter, and capabilities to do research that we could not have done before.

Of course, that is not a comfortable guarantee that nothing will change. Some tasks that might feel core to your identity as a researcher will be automated. Whether that leads to some time to breathe, think and explore, or just to more pressure to produce more ... that is an unresolved institutional question that calls for dialogue and collective action.

Sitting this out will be tough, though. AI is already raising the floor for quality and productivity, so that we will have to learn to navigate our way in that changing academy.

**Risks worth considering**

A few concerns are worth naming, even if I can only raise them as questions here.

*Deskilling.* If we rely on automation, we risk losing the ability to step in when needed. Airlines came to realise that auto-pilots were a great tool, but that pilots needed to regularly fly manually to maintain the skills for emergencies. In research, we might arrive at different solutions - maybe it's ok to lose the ability to write analysis code as long as we adopt effective verification practices - but that needs to happen deliberately.

*The talent pipeline.* Entry-level work is easiest to automate, but we need a pipeline. I'll be honest: it's becoming frustrating to spend more time briefing and supporting a junior researcher on a task than an AI agent would need to complete it - and then still need to review the output carefully (though the expected time saving from AI does not always materialise either). That frustration is worth talking about openly, because it's widespread and pretending it doesn't exist won't help junior researchers. But to keep academia thriving, and inclusive, we need to sustain an apprenticeship model. To what extent this consists in coaching junior researchers to use AI effectively versus getting them to do automatable work manually for the sake of learning is a question I'm torn about, and one that merits wider discussion.

*Volume.* If we can all put out many more papers, we will break a publishing system that relies on volunteer peer review, and our ability to keep up even within a narrow niche. Similarly, if funding applications become cheaper to produce, they'll increasingly be evaluated by AI and face ever lower odds. For the moment, I think it's essential for all of us to avoid adding pressure on the system unless we believe the output is actually valuable.

*AGI.* The prospect of "super-intelligence" is worth thinking about. We are not there yet (though credible people argue that we are very close). However, if we get to a point where autonomous AI systems can complete complex projects across domains better than human experts, this will raise fundamental questions about governance, economic redistribution and plainly the point of human existence. This is something that currently opens whole new research fields and certainly does not yet make us redundant ... but it is worth keeping an eye on.

**Staying current without burning out**

Those are big-picture concerns. Day to day, the more practical challenge is keeping up. New YouTube videos and headlines continuously promote the new best model of all time (and they're usually right). But most changes are incremental - a slightly better performance on benchmarks that may or may not relate to the work you actually do. Paradigm shifts are rarer, though we've seen a fair few: transformers, chat interfaces, reasoning modes, agents. Each fundamentally changes *what is possible* rather than simply shifting the boundaries.

For staying oriented without drowning in hype, find 2-3 sources you trust and ignore the noise. Ethan Mollick's *One Useful Thing* newsletter covers practical AI-at-work developments well. Other academics share lessons learned using AI for research in their specific discipline. Journals, unfortunately, are so slow that methods papers are often outdated as they are published, but a well scoped Google Scholar alert that includes preprints might be helpful.

## Try this: quarterly calibration

If you want to stay at the frontier, it makes sense to collect tasks where AI struggles - some where it makes a solid attempt but does not achieve the quality you would need for it to be useful, and some where it plainly fails. You might have come across both situations in the past few weeks?

Having taken note of these, specify what counts as a "pass" with specific checkable criteria. See how the current best model (typically in an appropriate agentic framework) performs against the task, and take specific notes. Then set yourself a quarterly reminder to try again. If the models pass, it's time to update your operating procedure (which you'll draft on Monday).

Just to give you examples of what such tasks might look like: For me, a key task that models have passed in recent months is to write full analysis code from a pre-registration and sample data, including enough documentation and tests for me to be able to verify it efficiently. Conversely, a task that I would like to automate but have not yet managed is the tasteful selection of illustrations for slides - that remains to be retested with new models.
